## What this reader needs decided

They are almost never the person who decides whether to buy the asset. They are the person
who has to **stand behind the number in front of someone else** — an investment committee, an
auditor, a limited partner, a regulator. So their real question is not "how bad is it" but:

1. Can I put this in a disclosure and defend it if challenged?
2. Where is it weak, and will I be the one to discover that, or will the auditor?
3. What do I have to ask the asset team for that I do not have?

Write for the second reading, not the first. This reader will come back to the document
under pressure, looking for the sentence that qualifies the number they just quoted.

## Framing and vocabulary

They live in framework language and are usually fluent in it: physical versus transition,
acute versus chronic, the four pillars, short/medium/long term. Use it — it saves
translation, and it signals the work will map onto what they file.

But they are frequently **not** climate scientists, and the failure mode is the opposite of
condescension: a report that uses "percentile", "ensemble" and "scenario" without pinning
each one down leaves them unable to defend it. Define each term once, in place, in a
sentence they could repeat verbatim to their auditor.

Never make them derive anything. If a figure needs a caveat, the caveat goes next to the
figure, not in an appendix they will not reach under deadline.

## Metrics that lead

- **What can and cannot be filed.** The vulnerable-asset count is the metric this reader
  expects to lead with, and we do not currently report it — the method is deferred. Say that
  first, plainly, with the reason: it is the single thing they most need to know before they
  start drafting, and discovering it late is worse than hearing it early.
- Exposure level and portfolio rank, which are what we can support.
- The list of what was **not** assessed. To this reader that is not a weakness in the
  report — it is the section that protects them, and burying it does them harm.
- Trend direction and its robustness, because "is it getting worse" is the question the
  committee asks after the level.

## Known confounders

- They may already have a physical-risk vendor whose numbers disagree with ours. Different
  hazard definitions, resolutions and baselines produce different answers legitimately, and
  the report should make ours checkable rather than assert primacy.
- They may be under a deadline set by someone else, which biases them toward taking a number
  and moving. Make the qualifications impossible to skip past.
- Their organisation may already have published time horizons and materiality thresholds.
  Ours must be replaced by theirs, not argued with.

## Questions for the customer

- Which framework are you filing against, and when is it due?
- Have you published definitions of short, medium and long term? What are they?
- Do you have an existing physical-risk assessment we should reconcile against?
- Who challenges this internally, and what did they push back on last time?
- What is your materiality threshold, and is it set in financial or operational terms?

## Do not claim

- Do not present a screening result as an audit-ready metric without naming what would have
  to be true for it to be one.
- Do not omit the limitations section to make the document read more confidently. This
  reader's exposure is personal, and a confident report that fails under challenge is worse
  for them than a qualified one.
